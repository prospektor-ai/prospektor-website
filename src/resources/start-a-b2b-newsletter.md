Start a B2B newsletter as a reason to email the people you want to sell to, and
judge it by the conversations it books with them. Ask one named buyer at a time
to be featured in an issue: a feature interview is a request with nothing for
sale in it, strangers answer it, and the interview is a sales conversation as
well. Fix one format with a word budget, publish weekly for eight issues, send
it from a person, and write down before issue one how few conversations will
make you stop.

The example below is one plan for a weekly newsletter aimed at the people who
own new business at B2B agencies. Put your own buyer in their place and the
method stays the same.

## Subscribers are the wrong measure

The usual advice is to grow a list and launch into it. [Three founders who
launched that way](/resources/network-beats-audience/), two of them to audiences
of twenty to thirty thousand, got two customers, a plateau at $7,000 to $8,000
of monthly recurring revenue, and a stall near $1,500. An audience built by a
founder talking about building also attracts the wrong reader: other founders,
price sensitive, quick to churn and mostly trying out new tools.

Email is [a relationship platform](/resources/the-channel-mix-changed/), and
the list is yours. Its size is still the wrong number to judge it by. A
newsletter platform's own growth features, such as recommendation swaps with
other publications, work by volume, and volume brings in exactly the reader you
do not sell to. The plan put the trade as a sum: two hundred agency owners beat
five thousand founders, and the second number is the one that feels like
progress. So leave the subscriber count out of every update: in the plan's
words, it is the metric that will "quietly replace the real one".

## A publication is a reason to email a stranger

"Can I show you my product?" goes unanswered. "I write a weekly piece on how B2B
agencies win new business. Can I feature your desk?" is a legitimate, flattering
ask, and strangers answer it.

The feature interview and a customer conversation are the same twenty minutes.
One request produces an issue and a conversation with a buyer, so the issues are
the by-product and the conversations are the output. Ask what you would ask on
[any first call](/resources/the-first-call-is-research/), and for the feature,
ask how they run new business: where the list comes from, what gets sent and
what they have stopped doing.

The reason to bother is arithmetic. A kit for forty first conversations, working
backwards from rates it marked as assumptions, put cold email at 4 to 8% from
send to conversation. At that rate forty conversations need 500 to 1,000
researched sends, which over four weeks is 125 to 250 a week while also holding
the calls. The kit concluded that the constraint was finding people to talk
to, and that the answer was warmer ways in rather than more cold volume. A
feature request is one of them.

The newsletter tests your words as well. Each issue reaches the segment you sell
to, so a phrase that gets replies there can move to your website after it has
been tried. That costs nothing, where [paid ads buy the same
iteration](/resources/ads-as-a-positioning-lab/) with a budget you can lose.

## Write the feature request before issue one

A twenty-minute call takes about two weeks to land in a calendar, so send the
first three requests on day one, before the first issue ships. Here is the
plan's request, with the sign-off left as a placeholder:

> **Subject:** Your new-business desk: 20 minutes?
>
> Hi {First name},
>
> I write a weekly teardown for people who own new business at B2B agencies: one
> company a week, the research a first meeting deserves and almost never gets.
>
> Every third issue features how one agency runs its new-business desk: where
> the list comes from, what gets sent, what they have stopped doing. I would
> like that to be {Agency}.
>
> Twenty minutes, your words, and you see the piece before it goes out. {One
> specific line about why them: a client win, a niche, something they
> published.}
>
> Worth a slot?
>
> {Sender}

Three rules for sending it. Personalise the line about why them or do not send
it, because an unpersonalised version is a worse cold email than an honest one.
Send it from a personal address, never from a marketing tool. Log every reply as
it lands, because each call is one of the conversations you are counting.

A yes gives you an issue, a conversation, a named relationship and a quote to
open the next request with. A no costs one email and often returns a referral.

## One format, with a word budget

Choose at most three formats and let one of them carry most issues. The plan had
a spine, a feature every third issue, and a short piece built on one number for
a week that runs short.

Its spine was a teardown of one real company: the research someone should have
done before a first meeting with them. Seven sections, always in this order,
each with a budget:

| Section | What goes in it | Words |
|---|---|---|
| The line | Name, what they do, one number | 30 |
| Why now | The trigger, with dates | 120 |
| The one thing | The single fact the approach rests on | 30 |
| Two signals for, one against | The against makes the other two believable | 220 |
| Who to approach | Roles, because a name needs a source, and why that seat owns the problem | 120 |
| The opening line | The sentence you would send | 80 |
| Sources | Plain links | links |

The reader gets the verdict before the evidence and the evidence before the
caveats. An issue runs 600 to 900 words, and a teardown that runs to 1,400 is a
different product and a worse one. Everything in it is checkable, or it does not
run, and the against is the section that gets forwarded.

Pick each subject by four tests, and require all four. A reader recognises the
name without being told who they are. There is a dated public trigger in the
last 90 days: funding, a hire, a launch or an acquisition. Your reader could
plausibly sell to them. Nothing in the piece needs a source you cannot link.
For the first issue, which every later subscriber reads first, a company with a
boring, well-documented trigger beats an exciting one that rests on a claim you
cannot link.

## Weekly, eight issues, and the stop decided now

Publish weekly, on a fixed day. The plan chose Tuesday and called fortnightly
"the lie a busy operator tells themselves". Name all eight issues before the
first one ships, because a queue decided in advance is what stops a weekly
publication dying on the question of what to write this week.

Then set the stopping rule while it is cheap. The plan's: fewer than six
conversations with agency owners traceable to the newsletter by issue eight, and
it has failed as a way to find people to talk to, so stop or change the request.
Subscribers appear nowhere in that rule. Make the eighth issue the review, and
publish the decision in it.

## Set it up so people answer

Say who it is for. Naming the buyer gets more replies from the right people and
fewer readers overall, which is the right trade when the goal is booked calls.
"I write about how B2B agencies win new business" gets a reply, and "I write
about go-to-market" does not. The line under the newsletter's name names the
format and the cadence and claims no category. The plan's was *The research a
first meeting deserves. One company, every Tuesday.*

- **Send it from a person.** Use a first name and a surname as the sender, never
  the company. A newsletter from a person gets opened, and one from a brand gets
  filed. Set the reply address to an inbox you read, because the replies are the
  point.
- **End the welcome email on one question.** It is the one email every
  subscriber opens. The plan's asks *What are you trying to win right now?* and
  invites the reader to name the account, or the type of account, because
  subjects are picked from the replies. A reply is a conversation, and the
  answers fill the queue with companies your buyers care about.
- **Test delivery before issue one.** Send yourself a draft, open it in the two
  mail systems your buyers use at work, check it lands in the inbox rather than
  a promotions or junk folder, and read it on a phone.
- **Write issue one before you announce anything.** An empty publication
  converts nobody.
- **Leave out a paid tier.** A cheap subscription beside the thing you sell
  competes with it for the same attention and attracts the price-sensitive
  reader.
- **Give the newsletter original writing.** If the platform will not let you set
  a canonical URL, republishing an article that already ranks on your site
  points a high-authority duplicate at your own page.

## Five things

1. **Judge it on conversations booked with named buyers**, and leave the
   subscriber count out of every update.
2. **Ask one buyer at a time to be featured**, with three requests sent on day
   one.
3. **Fix one format with a word budget**, and a linkable source for every claim.
4. **Publish weekly for eight issues**, named in advance, with the stopping rule
   written down before the first.
5. **Send it from a person**, and end the welcome email on a question.
