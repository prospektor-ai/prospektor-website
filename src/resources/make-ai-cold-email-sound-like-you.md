To make AI-drafted cold email sound like you, give the tool your own writing to
copy and your own rules to obey, and keep the last word on every sentence that
sends. Write the first emails yourself the way you would send them, keep your
standing rules in one place where every draft reads them, read each draft
before it goes with the reason for its opening line beside it, and change the
words by hand rather than asking for a rewrite.

What the email should say is a separate job, covered in [personalisation that
works](/resources/cold-email-personalisation/): the evidence about the recipient
that only someone who looked could know. This page starts once a tool has
written the draft and the question is whether it reads like you.

## How buyers spot AI email

The people who use these tools are the ones who can tell, and they name three
failures.

**Everyone's email has the same structure.** One buyer of these tools put the
giveaway simply: AI email all follows the same sales structure, so a reader can
tell where it came from.

**The personalisation picks the wrong detail.** The same buyer found that every
attempt went off on its own and personalised to something odd that no person
would mention. In one outbound tool's user community, a member
found that template language had bled into the emails written for individual
leads, which *"ended up including wrong/irrelevant details"*.

**One small change rewrites the rest.** A marketer in the same community wanted
to review the copy and make a few minor edits, and found the tool rewriting all
of it: *"If I change one thing, it goes and changes something else, like the
subject line."* Another member put it as a preference: *"I do not want to prompt
AI for everything and every small change."*

Complaints about the copy, and requests to edit it by hand, came up about
twenty-five times in the messages read from that community.

## Write the first emails yourself

When members complained about the AI copy, that tool's own staff answered with
a method: *"writing out emails the way you would personally send them"*,
saving that where the tool keeps its knowledge, and having it work from that
template. A member who was not happy with the final copy went further: *"I'd
rather write the hook of every email and make it VERY personal."*

So before a tool drafts anything, write a few emails yourself, to real
prospects, exactly as you would send them. Give the tool those as the pattern.
They carry the structure you like your emails to have, and structure is what
the buyer above said gives AI email away. If you want a structure you did not
invent, choose one on purpose, such as [a four-email sequence that expects no
reply to the first email](/resources/cold-email-sequence/), and write your
examples in it.

## Keep your standing rules in one place

Some corrections apply to every email you will ever send. Write them down once,
where every draft reads them, instead of typing them again into each request.

Sellers who get usable drafts work this way. One described the instructions
the best users give as paragraphs long, all saying the same few things: stay in
bounds, do not guess, do not use AI language. Their own version was a paragraph
of do not guess, no AI language and no em dashes, pasted into every prompt, with
a manual review step after it. By their own estimate that got the drafts most of
the way, and not all of it.

Your list will be your own, and it has three parts:

- **No guessing.** Nothing about the prospect that is not in the material you
  gave the tool.
- **The language you never use.** The phrases, the punctuation and the openers
  you would not write yourself.
- **The format you do use.** How you open, how long you run, how you ask and how
  you sign off.

Keep it in one file you paste into every request, or in the place your tool
keeps standing instructions.

## Read every draft before it sends

The seller with the paragraph of rules still kept a manual review step. The
member who would rather write their own hooks described the alternative:
pressing launch, which felt like saying yes to copy that read as weird, and then
editing the messages one at a time, without *"an easy view to check on why in
the first place I got this hook"* or the profile of the person it was for. They
asked for the fix: let the user change the message, and add it to the schedule
only when it is good enough.

Read each draft with two things beside it:

- **The reason for the opening line.** What fact about this person or company
  the hook rests on, and where it came from. If the tool cannot say, write the
  hook yourself.
- **The person it is for.** Their name, role and company, so you can see the
  draft greets the right person at the [company your list says it
  is](/resources/clean-a-prospect-list/).

Then read it once more for anything carried over: a name, a detail or a claim
that belongs to another prospect or to the template. That is the bleed the
member above found.

## Change the word and keep the sentence

When a draft is close, edit it yourself. Asking the tool for a small change is
how the marketer above had their subject line changed. Change the word, keep the
sentence, and leave the rest alone.

Keep your edited copy somewhere of your own. In the same community, a member who
had written custom messages for each lead asked for one small change to the
sequence's timing, and it wiped all of them and put the generic message back,
*"Which now can't be undone."*

Choose tools that let you edit the text directly. Another member, finding
that no prompt fixed the copy, wrote: *"PLEASE BRING BACK MANUAL EDITING FOR
CAMPAIGNS."*

## Turn a repeated correction into a rule

When you find yourself making the same correction again, add it to your
standing rules, so the next draft arrives without it. The buyer quoted above
asked for exactly this: a tool that learns from their feedback. Until your tool does
that, do it by hand: keep a note of what you change, and read the note when you
update the rules.

## Five things

1. **Write the first emails yourself**, exactly as you would send them, and give
   them to the tool as the pattern.
2. **Keep your standing rules in one place**: no guessing, the language you
   never use, the format you do.
3. **Read every draft before it sends**, beside the reason for its opening line
   and the person it is for.
4. **Change the word and keep the sentence**, and keep your own copy of the edit.
5. **Make a repeated correction a rule**, so the next draft does not need it.
