Ask for a warm introduction by sending the connector two things: a short note
saying who you want to meet and why, and underneath it a finished email, under a
hundred words, that they can forward to that person as it stands. Choose the
connector by how well they know the target, and give them an easy way to say no.

## Why introductions stall

Most people you ask for an introduction want to help. A company that builds
software for warm introductions puts the diagnosis plainly in its own guide:
finding the right connector is the easy half, and what stops most connectors is
the time it takes to write the message.

Picture the request from their side. A note that reads *could you introduce me to
someone at Northwind?* hands them a writing job. They have to work out what you
do, why Northwind would care, how to describe you without embarrassing
themselves, and how to put all of it in their own voice. That is real work for a
favour, so it goes on the list of things to do later, and later rarely comes.

The same note with a finished email underneath it is the same favour with the work
already done. They read it, change a word if they want to, and press send.

## The two parts of the ask

**The note to the connector** is for them alone. It says who you want to meet,
why now, and that the draft below is theirs to change or ignore. It can be plain
about what you want, because the connector already knows you.

**The forwardable email** is written in the connector's voice and addressed to the
person you want to meet. It is the message the connector would write if they had
the time, which is exactly why you write it for them. The same guide gives four
rules for it, and each one holds up:

- **Under a hundred words.** The target reads it from someone they know and
  decides in a few seconds whether to reply.
- **Name the shared context.** How the connector knows you, and why they thought
  of the target. That line is what makes the introduction warm. Without it the
  email is a cold one with a friend's name on top.
- **Be specific about the ask.** Twenty minutes about one named thing. *Would love
  to connect* leaves the target to work out what you want, and most will not try.
- **Give the connector an easy out.** Say in your note that a no costs nothing and
  that you will not chase. A request that is comfortable to refuse is comfortable
  to accept, and it spares the connector a choice between an awkward refusal and
  silence.

## What it looks like written down

The whole ask, both parts, with the placeholders you fill in:

> **To the connector**
>
> {Name}, I'd like to meet {target} at {company}. They've just {a specific,
> checkable thing they did}, and that's the problem we work on. You know them from
> {shared context}, so I thought I'd ask you first.
>
> I've drafted something below so you don't have to write it. Change anything, or
> tell me now isn't a good time. Either is fine, and I won't chase.
>
> **For {Name} to forward**
>
> {Target}, {sender} runs {company}, which {one line on what it does, in the
> target's terms}. We know each other from {shared context}. Given {the thing they
> did}, I thought the two of you should talk. Would you be open to twenty minutes
> in the next couple of weeks? If it's not for you, no reply needed.

The forward runs to about sixty words. Three things in it are doing the work:

- **The checkable thing.** It tells the connector why the target will welcome the
  email, and it tells the target you looked. It follows the rule from
  [personalisation that works in a cold email](/resources/cold-email-personalisation/):
  a sentence the reader could confirm or correct.
- **The shared context, in the connector's words.** It is the only line a cold
  email cannot contain.
- **The exit for the target.** *No reply needed* protects the connector too. If
  the target is not interested, nobody owes anybody a follow-up.

## Ask the strongest tie, whatever their title

The instinct is to route the request through the most impressive person you know
who knows the target: the investor, the old boss, the board member. The same guide
says the opposite, and it is right: choose the strongest connector, not the most
senior one. A grand name with a thin tie produces a lukewarm forward, when it
produces one at all.

Strength is easier to judge than it sounds. The rules one relationship-scoring
product publishes for its own scores work just as well on your contacts:

- **Two-way history is the signal.** Someone you have written back and forth with,
  recently or often, is a strong tie.
- **One-way traffic counts for nothing.** If someone has emailed you a hundred
  times and you never replied, you do not know them. This rule is what keeps
  newsletters, vendors and cold sequences off your list of connectors.
- **A thread with fifty people on it proves nothing.** Being copied on the same
  big email chain says nothing about whether either of you would take a call
  from the other.
- **A bare connection is weak by default.** Being connected on LinkedIn does not
  mean you know someone. A message history, or email in both directions, is what
  moves them up.
- **Correct by hand for the friend you never email.** The person you see every
  month and never write to is invisible to every signal above. You know they are
  strong, so count them.

Run that over the people who know your target, and ask the one at the top.

## When the ask should come from someone else

Sometimes the connector knows your cofounder or your investor far better than they
know you. Then the request should come from that person, even if you wrote it. One
warm-introduction product builds an approval step for exactly this case: a salesperson
drafts the request, the CEO sends it to the board member who knows the target, and
the board member sees a request from someone they know well instead of from a
stranger on the sales team. The same holds in a company of three. Whoever on your
side has the strongest tie to the connector sends the note.

## When you have no path at all

Sometimes nobody you know knows the target. Then the ask changes shape: you ask
your network for a kind of person rather than a named one, and you keep the
request as small as it can be. A version written for a founder trying to meet
agency owners:

> {Name}, I'm spending four weeks talking to agency owners about whether the thing
> I've built is useful to them, before I build anything else. Not selling. I want
> to be told it's useless while that's still cheap.
>
> Does anyone in your world run a B2B agency that does lead generation,
> partnerships or business development for its clients? A forwarded sentence is
> all I need; I'll do the rest.

Send these on day one of any outreach push, before the first cold email.
Introductions are the slowest conversations to arrive, because each one waits on
somebody else's inbox before it reaches yours.

All of this assumes a network worth asking, which is [the case for building one
instead of an audience](/resources/network-beats-audience/). Where introductions
sit among the other channels, and when they are the right one, is
[where your buyers are](/resources/fish-where-the-fish-are/).

## Five things

1. **Write the forwardable email yourself**, in the connector's voice, under a
   hundred words.
2. **Put the shared context and one specific ask in it.** Those two lines are the
   introduction.
3. **Give the connector an easy out, in writing**, and keep your word about not
   chasing.
4. **Pick the connector by two-way history**, never by title.
5. **Send introduction requests first.** They take the longest to come back.
